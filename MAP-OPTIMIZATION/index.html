    Designing maps for mirror performance¶

    Chivalry 2 is competitive, and many players run 144-240+ FPS. Every live mirror renders the scene again, so a mirror-heavy map can lose a lot of frame rate. This guide covers what a map designer can do about it. The advice is based on measurements on MirrorMaze, listed at the end.

    The one rule: count captures, not mirrors¶

    Each time a live mirror updates, it renders the scene once. That is a capture. Frame time grows with the number of captures per frame. Mirror size and render-target resolution matter much less.

    • Measured: roughly 0.2-0.3 ms per capture on the test PC (Resolution 0.35-0.5). FPS followed captures-per-frame second by second.
    • Lowering resolution from 0.5 to 0.35 saved only about 0.14 ms per frame once captures were reduced.

    Everything below reduces captures, makes each capture cheaper, or spreads captures evenly across frames.

    1. Bake your lighting (biggest single gain)¶

    Movable lights are recalculated in every view, so each mirror capture redoes the lighting. With static (baked) lights, walls and floors read precomputed light maps, which is cheap in the main view and in every capture.

    • Measured: +23% walking, +31% standing (243 → 298 and 239 → 313 FPS on the test PC). Captures per frame stayed the same; each one just got cheaper.
    • How:
    • Set lights to Static.
    • In World Settings, turn off Force No Precomputed Lighting.
    • Make sure meshes have a lightmap UV channel.
    • Build lighting.
    • Set decoration meshes without lightmap UVs (unlit trims, decals, floor guides) to Movable.
    • Shadows on static lights are free at runtime. Turning them on gives real dark corners and contact shadows.
    • Large single meshes (a whole floor or ceiling) get coarse light maps that show as soft squares and grain. Some find this adds character; split the mesh into tiles if you want it smooth.
    • Trade-offs: lights cannot change in game. Players are lit from stored light samples rather than direct light, so they look slightly flatter.
    • General baking pitfall, not mirror-specific: overlapping faces in the same plane (for example a wall running into a pillar of equal width) flicker once baked.
    • Do not try to switch lights off only inside mirrors. Chivalry 2 is a shipping build, and the engine ignores light-type show flags there.

    2. Use fewer, longer mirrors¶

    A wall built from several mirror pieces captures once per piece. One mirror covering the same flat run captures once.

    • Measured on a 463-piece maze:
    Merge Mirrors Walking FPS Standing FPS
    None 463 223 220
    Touching pieces merged 326 246 231
    Also merged across pillar and junction gaps 243 252 258

    Merging across pillar gaps cut captures per frame by 25-35%. - Pieces must lie in the same plane, face the same way and share their settings. - A mirror may run behind a pillar: the pillar hides that part, and reflections still show the pillar. - Do not run a mirror behind the end of a wall that meets the mirror face. The wall end sits inside the mirror's render-panel layer (the first few centimetres behind the face) and flickers, which is most visible with baked shadows. Split the mirror at such junctions instead. - Very long mirrors spread one render target over more wall, so they look slightly softer. Resolution 0.5 compensates cheaply.

    3. Update every other frame, staggered¶

    • UpdateEveryNFrames = 2 halves capture work. Measured: about −2.5 to −3.3 ms per frame (136 → 208 FPS walking at the time). Fast motion in reflections is slightly less smooth; testers noticed it only when looking for it.
    • On its own, every mirror updates on the same frame, so frames alternate heavy and light, and the worst frames (1% low) do not improve.
    • Add StaggerMode = Distance (or Distributed) to spread mirrors across frames. Measured: 1% low improved from about 110 to 138 FPS walking (187 standing), with the best average as well.
    • Staggering is phase-based: (FrameCounter + phase) % N == 0, with the phase taken from the mirror's position. Since 0.6.0 the phase hash spreads grid-aligned mirrors evenly (the old one clustered them on one phase).
    • Large maps can be CPU-bound on Blueprint ticks. On MirrorMaze (263 mirrors) the off-screen test cost more processor time than it saved: turning it off gave +52 FPS standing and +32 walking, so 0.6.0 removed it. Captures had become cheap (about 0.085 ms each). Keep the lean tick (ChecksEveryNFrames 4, the default).

    3b. Recommended schedule for mirror-heavy maps¶

    Since 0.6.0 these are the defaults of every mirror (plus Resolution 0.5, StaggerMode Distance and DisableReflectionAO on). Change them map-wide with BP_MirrorKitSettings (per-mirror settings) and BP_MirrorKitBudget (the budget rows below); see Settings for many mirrors at once in the map-making guide.

    Use time, not frames, and let a budget trim the far mirrors:

    Setting Value Why
    TargetUpdateRate 90 ~every 3 frames at 300 FPS, every frame at 60 FPS (frame-based every-2 is only 30 updates/s at a 60 FPS cap; testers saw it as choppy)
    CaptureBudget 2 captures per frame across the map; stretches far, off-centre mirrors when the load is higher
    BudgetFocusDistance / BudgetFocusAngle 800 cm / 15° (defaults) mirrors near you or near the view centre keep their pace while FocusExemptFromBudget is on (without focus, budgets looked choppy in bouts)
    MinUpdateRate 30 the budget never pushes a mirror below 30 updates/s (at a 60 FPS cap that means at most every 2nd frame)
    FocusUpdateRate 144 focused mirrors (near or central) update faster, against sideways jitter at high uncapped FPS (free below about 216 FPS, where 144/s is already every frame)

    Measured on MirrorMaze (263 mirrors after merging): 327 FPS walking uncapped (every-2 without budget: ~291; no mirrors: ~379), 1% low ~207. At a 60 FPS cap, mirrors update 30-60 times a second at ~36 % GPU, and testers saw them as smooth. Keep TargetUpdateRate well below your usual FPS: with rounding, a mirror drops to every frame only below 1.5 × the rate.

    3c. Smoothness at high frame rates¶

    Rate-based schedules make a mirror update every round(FPS / rate) frames, so the faster the PC, the more frames each reflection is held. Below about 135 FPS the default 90 updates/s is already every frame; jitter is a high-FPS question.

    Finding Measured Setting
    Sideways strafing next to a mirror looked jittery uncapped (~300 FPS) walking: no focus 355, focus 144/s 315, focus every frame 281 FPS; 144 greatly reduced it, every frame removed it FocusUpdateRate 144 by default; players/hosts raise it with -MirrorFocusRate=1000 / ?MirrorFocusRate=1000
    The interval flipped between N and N+1 with frame-time noise near rounding boundaries, giving uneven gaps smoothed interval visibly steadier at ~0-3 % FPS uncapped, no cost at a 240 cap; an anchored cadence added no visible gain for ~2-5 % SmoothUpdateInterval on (default), AnchorUpdateCadence optional
    Close first-person strafing still slightly choppy at 144/s above ~216 FPS confirmed by -MirrorFocusRate=1000 (not resolution, not tick order) default kept at 144 by choice; Ultra preset = every frame

    0.6.0 also made the per-mirror Blueprint work cheaper (interval stored once per tick; one hidden manager for FPS smoothing, budget and quality). On MirrorMaze, same map and settings, uncapped, these changes alone gave +7 % standing (360 -> 385 FPS), +3 % walking (320 -> 330) and better 1 % lows.

    Judge smoothness on the display you play on: on a 60 Hz screen, uncapped FPS mainly lowers input latency, and a 60 (or 120/180) cap gives the cleanest picture because every mirror then updates every frame.

    4. Settings worth knowing¶

    Setting Effect measured Visual cost Recommendation
    Resolution 0.5 → 0.35 about +3% (8 FPS walking) after the steps above softer reflections 0.5 is a good default; drop only if GPU-bound with many mirrors
    DisableReflectionAO about −1 ms standing in early tests (every-frame updates); overlaps with resolution not noticeable in mirrors on by default
    CaptureDistance 15 m −1.75 ms walking distant mirrors visibly freeze prefer staggering and merging; keep the default 25 m unless the layout hides distant mirrors
    MaxViewDistance small (+6-9 FPS at 25-15 m from a ~40 FPS baseline, older kit) removes distant geometry from reflections rarely worth it in closed layouts
    Partners on (partnered mirrors) each mirror of a working pair renders one extra inner reflection, at half its update rate and sized to the screen tunnel effect use only where the effect is the point; off in every preset except Omega

    CaptureDistance and DistanceStaggerStart measure to the nearest point of the mirror face, so long mirrors stay live near their ends.

    5. Layout habits¶

    • Corners where two mirrors face each other keep both live even when one is only visible inside the other.
    • Mirrors behind the player, or outside the view, still count while they appear in other reflections; mirrors drawn nowhere stop after VisibilityTolerance.
    • Mirrors inside the view but hidden behind walls can still capture when they appear in a reflection; the engine does not tell Blueprints about main-view occlusion. Breaking long sight lines between mirrors helps.
    • Decorative surfaces that do not need a live player reflection should use baked tiles (BP_MirrorTile) or M_MirrorEnvironment instead of live mirrors.

    6. Partners and reflection size (kit 0.8.5-0.8.7, measured on Mirror Maze with 20 partner pairs)¶

    Same route, uncapped, single runs each (identical builds differ by about 7 % on the mean):

    Kit FPS mean p5 1 % low worst dip
    0.8.2 (fixed-size reflections) 312 284 193 155
    0.8.6 (sized to screen coverage) 330-353 265-298 218-239 111-120
    0.8.7 (inner reflection on its own frame) 379 332 260 173
    0.8.7 with FocusUpdateRate 90 408 368 285 205
    • Sizing reflections to screen coverage helps every mirror, partnered or not: small and distant mirrors get small targets.
    • A lower in-game resolution scale raises the average but not the dips, which are not about pixel count.
    • Partner walls you stand near are "focused" (they skip the budget and update at FocusUpdateRate, 144/s by default). That is a large share of their steady cost. 90/s cuts it further, at the risk of slight sideways judder above about 200 FPS.
    • The deepest dips came from a pair's two renders landing in one frame. Since 0.8.7, the inner reflection renders at half rate on a frame of its own; it did not look choppier in testing.
    • One-off ~1 s dips (worst moment about a third of the average FPS) remain with mirrors, partners or not; a map without mirrors has only shallower ones. Counting focused mirrors in the budget (FocusExemptFromBudget off) lifts the average 3-6 % but not these dips.
    • Known limits: a resized reflection leaves its old render target to garbage collection (freeing it at once would stall rendering), so video memory can run above the live targets until the next collection; and an inner reflection queued for its own frame can still render once after you look away (at most a few frames later).

    How these numbers were measured¶

    • Map: MirrorMaze (463 mirror pieces at first, 263 after merging coplanar pieces; 81 point lights), one PC, solo, frame-rate cap off.
    • Machine: GPU NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 (8 GB, driver 32.0.16.1074 = 610.74); CPU Intel Core i7-14700F (20 cores / 28 threads); 32 GB RAM; Windows 11 Home (build 26200). Game: 1920 × 1080 fullscreen, VSync off, sg.* quality 3 (Epic) except foliage and shading 1, resolution scale 100 %. Values read on 2026-10-17 from Windows and GameUserSettings.ini; earlier measurements are assumed to use the same machine.
    • Recording: NVIDIA App performance logs. Each case is recorded as 30 s standing at spawn plus one fixed walking route, repeated in alternating order (2-3 rounds).
    • Telemetry: captures per frame come from each mirror's CaptureCount, read by test tools. Absolute FPS differs per PC; use the relative changes.
